# The spreadsheet that saved us $200,000

*No vendor, no big project. One analyst, data we already had, and 80% accuracy.*

Every morning at 7, a spreadsheet tells us which trucks are likely to be late. Not today. **Two days from now.**

It's right about 80% of the time. Last year it saved us **roughly $200,000 in late-delivery penalties.**

A junior analyst on our ops team built it. Nobody asked her to.

[Subscribe button]

## The problem we kept paying for

Late trucks are expensive for us. Many of our customer contracts carry penalties when a delivery window is missed.

For years, we found out about delays the same way everyone does: the truck was late, a customer called, and we scrambled. By then the penalty was already locked in. **We were great at reacting and bad at seeing it coming.**

We'd talked about buying a prediction tool. The quotes were big, and the projects were long. So it sat on the "someday" list.

## What she built

Our analyst got tired of watching the same lanes run late. So she started pulling data we already had into one sheet:

- Each carrier's on-time history, by lane
- Dock appointment times at our busiest sites
- Recent delays on the same route
- A few outside signals, like weather alerts

Then she built a simple score. Each shipment gets a risk rating for two days out. High scores go on a short list.

**No machine learning platform. No new vendor.** Formulas, lookups, and a lot of patient checking against what actually happened.

## Why two days matters

A delay you learn about today is a crisis. A delay you learn about two days early is a scheduling problem.

With two days' notice, we can:

1. Rebook the load with a more reliable carrier
2. Move the dock appointment
3. Warn the customer and agree on a new window before the penalty kicks in

Each of those is cheap. Together, they add up to about $200,000 we didn't pay last year.

## How it went from side project to daily tool

She showed it to her manager after testing it quietly for a few weeks. Her manager brought it to me. My first question was how often it was right.

She had the answer ready: **about 80% of flagged shipments really did run late.** That was enough. We didn't need perfect. We needed better than finding out from an angry customer.

Now it runs every morning. The ops team checks the list before their first call of the day. When it's wrong, we note why, and she tunes the score.

## What I took from it

**Simple tools that ship beat smart tools that don't.** We spent a year discussing a big system. A spreadsheet fixed most of the problem in a few weeks.

**Junior people see problems seniors have stopped seeing.** She hadn't been here long enough to think late trucks were just normal.

**The best question in ops is "what would we do with more notice?"** If the answer is "a lot," it's worth trying to predict, even roughly.
