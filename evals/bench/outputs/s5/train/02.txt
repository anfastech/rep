# 41% of our tickets came from 3 screens

*We thought support needed more people. It needed better onboarding screens.*

Last spring our support lead asked me for two more hires. The queue kept growing, response times were slipping, and the team was tired.

It was a fair ask. Before I said yes, I wanted to know what was actually filling the queue. So we pulled **18 months of support tickets** and read them.

[Subscribe button]

## How we looked

Most ticket reports sort by category: billing, bug, how-to, feature request. Those labels tell you what the customer asked. They don't tell you where they got stuck.

So we added one tag to every ticket: **the screen the customer was on when the problem started.** Two people spent most of a week on it. Boring work. Worth every hour.

We didn't need fancy tools. A spreadsheet export, a shared tag list, and a rule that if you weren't sure, you marked it "unknown" instead of guessing.

## What we found

When we sorted by screen, one pattern jumped out.

**41% of all tickets came from the same 3 onboarding screens.** Not the hard parts of the product. Not the complex routing features. Three setup screens every new customer sees in their first week.

The tickets were almost all the same shape:

- "What do I put in this field?"
- "I skipped this step. Now nothing works."
- "Is this setting for my warehouse or my carrier?"

None of these were bugs. The screens worked exactly as built. They just assumed the customer already knew what we knew.

## Why nobody had noticed

Each ticket on its own looked reasonable. A new customer asks a setup question. Support answers it. Ticket closed, customer happy.

Nobody saw the pile because **the pile was spread across 18 months and a dozen category labels.** Billing looked like billing. How-to looked like how-to. Only the screen tag showed that a big share of them started in the same three places.

That's the part that stung. We'd been paying for this problem every week and filing it under "normal support load."

## What we changed

We didn't redesign the product. We rewrote three screens. Here's what that meant in practice:

1. **One decision per screen.** One of the three asked customers to make four setup choices at once. We split it up.
2. **Plain words in every field label.** We swapped our internal terms for the words customers used in their tickets. Their tickets told us exactly what to call things.
3. **An example next to every field.** If a field needs a format, we show one filled in.
4. **A warning before skipping a required step.** One sentence telling people what breaks later if they skip it.

Product and support wrote the new copy together. Support knew the questions. Product knew the constraints. It took a few weeks, not a quarter.

## The result

After the new screens went live, **total ticket volume dropped by about a third.** Same customer growth. Same support team.

We didn't make those two hires. The team that was underwater now has room to work on the tickets that actually need a person.

[Share button]

* * *

The thing to remember: support tickets are product feedback with a timestamp. Sort them by where people got stuck, not just by what they asked.

## Try it yourself

You can run a small version of this in a week:

1. Export the last 6-12 months of tickets.
2. Tag each one with the screen or step where the problem started.
3. Sort by tag and look at the top three.

I'd bet one of them surprises you.

## Core voice characteristics
- Register: casual professional. Sounds like an ops lead talking in the hallway, not on a keynote stage.
- Avg sentence length: 11-14 words, with frequent short fragments.
- Humor: dry, mostly at her own expense.
- Confidence: declarative. Says what happened and what she does now. Hedges only on things she hasn't tested.

## Vocabulary
- Uses: sites, the floor, orders, rollout, the numbers, plain verbs (cut, ship, fix, kill, pull)
- Avoids: synergy, "leverage" as a verb, "excited to announce", game-changer, journey, exclamation marks, em dashes
- Technical level: assumes ops literacy; explains software terms in one clause

## Structural patterns
- Opens with: a specific moment or number, stated flat
- Closes with: one direct question to readers, signed "Maya"
- Paragraphs: 1-3 sentences
- Uses prose for stories; numbered lists only for steps

- Puts the number before the lesson: "41% of all tickets came from the same 3 onboarding screens."

- Bolds the one-line rule in each section so skimmers get the argument
