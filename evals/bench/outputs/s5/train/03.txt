# I cancelled every recurring meeting for a month

*22 meetings came off the calendar. Only 4 earned their way back.*

Here's an unpopular opinion: **most companies hold too many recurring meetings, and nobody notices because each one looks harmless.**

Thirty minutes here. A weekly sync there. Add them up and your best people spend a day a week reporting on work instead of doing it.

So I ran a test. For one month, I cancelled every recurring meeting in operations. All 22 of them.

[Subscribe button]

## Why I did it

Recurring meetings are easy to start and almost impossible to stop. Someone sets one up to solve a real problem. The problem goes away. The meeting stays.

Nobody cancels it because cancelling feels rude. Nobody skips it because skipping feels risky. So it runs forever on autopilot, like a truck route nobody remembers booking.

I wanted to know which of ours were still doing a job and which were just habit.

## The rules

I kept it simple:

1. **Every recurring meeting came off the calendar** on the same Monday. No exceptions, including mine.
2. **One-off meetings were fine.** If you needed to talk, you booked a call.
3. **To bring a meeting back, you had to write one paragraph.** What decision does it make, or what problem does it catch, that nothing else does?

That third rule did most of the work. It's easy to defend a meeting out loud. It's harder to write down what it's for.

## What came back

After a month, **4 of the 22 came back.**

- My 1:1s with direct reports. Nothing replaces them.
- The weekly site leads call, where problems cross sites and need a decision fast.
- The incident review, because it's where we actually change how we work.
- A monthly planning session, cut from 90 minutes to 45.

The other 18 didn't come back. Most turned into a short written update in a shared channel. A few just ended, and nobody missed them.

## What surprised me

I expected people to fight for their meetings. Some did, at first. But once the month started, **almost nobody asked for one back unprompted.**

The work didn't slow down. If anything, decisions got faster. When a meeting isn't sitting on the calendar, people solve the problem the day it shows up instead of saving it for Thursday.

And people noticed the time. A few site leads told me they got back three or four hours a week. That's half a shift.

## What I got wrong

It wasn't all clean. Two things broke:

**New hires lost a way to learn.** Some of those status meetings were where new people quietly picked up how things work. We added a short weekly onboarding session for anyone in their first 90 days.

**Some people felt cut off.** A meeting is also a signal that you're in the loop. Written updates fixed most of that, but only once we made them predictable: same day, same format, every week.

So my claim isn't "meetings are bad." It's that **a recurring meeting should have to justify itself, and most can't.**

[Share button]

* * *

The thing to remember: if a meeting can't explain in one paragraph what it decides, it's a habit, not a meeting.

## Run your own version

You don't need a full month or a whole department. Try this:

1. Pick one team and cancel every recurring meeting for two weeks.
2. Ask for one written paragraph to bring any of them back.
3. Write down what you actually missed.

Then look at what's left on the calendar.
